01 · The problem

The first plan is rarely the last one

A language model's first attempt at a plan usually looks reasonable — and usually breaks somewhere in the middle. Here, the robot is told to wash a load of laundry:

Walk to washing machine ✓ → Open it ✓ → Pick up soap ✓ → Pick up a dress ✓ → Pick up pants ✗

Both hands are already full. The plan didn't account for that — and without some way to recover, this is where the task simply ends.

Even given three separate tries with no diagnosis or repair — just plan, attempt, and try fresh on failure — that only succeeds 15–78% of the time, depending how demanding the task is. Everything from here is what closes that gap in a single, informed attempt.

A language model is only asked for input once in this loop, for a short hint at step 3. Steps 1, 2 and 4 are handled entirely by rule — the same rules the simulated house itself runs on.

03 · Step 1

Diagnose: what kind of failure is this?

The house doesn't explain why an action failed — so the system replays what happened and works it out. Three shapes of failure come up in practice:

  • 1
    A step was skipped Something the action needed was never done — e.g. trying to put an item away that was never picked up.
  • 2
    Steps happened out of order Everything needed did happen, just in the wrong sequence — e.g. both hands filled up before the plan tried to pick up a third item.
  • 3
    The object can't do that The action doesn't apply to this object at all — e.g. asking the character to lie down on something you can't lie on.
04 · Step 2

Tracing back to the real cause

The system replays the plan from the very beginning and watches, action by action, for the exact moment things stopped being true — not the step that just failed, but whatever happened earlier that actually caused it. Once it knows that, it picks how to respond:

Add the missing step
One small thing was skipped.
walk over, stand up, face the object
Patch this action
The object can't do what was asked.
try a close variation, same spot
Swap the action
The step itself was the wrong idea.
ask for a different one entirely
Already done
The goal is already true.
drop the now-pointless step
Rebuild the sequence
Everything else — more than one thing needs to change, or no single-step patch applies.
hands off to the full search, next
05 · Step 3

Searching for a fix

For the general case, the system explores possible next actions and keeps the shortest sequence that actually satisfies what's missing — checked directly against the same rules the house itself enforces. It has three sources of ideas to draw from:

  • 1
    Rule-based fixes Open a closed door, free up a hand, walk to the right spot — worked out directly from what's known about the objects involved, no guessing required.
  • 2
    A hint from the language model A short list of suggested actions naming what might fix it — treated as ideas to test, not something accepted on faith.
  • 3
    What the plan was already trying to do Keeps the search from losing track of steps it still needs to cover.
06 · Step 4

Weave it back in, then try again

The fix gets stitched into the original plan and the whole thing re-runs from the top. Three specific safeguards keep that stitching from causing new problems:

  • ✓
    Put things where they belong If both hands are full, the fix now delivers a held item to wherever it was actually supposed to end up, instead of just dropping it on the floor.
  • ✓
    Reopen what got closed If something needed is now stuck behind a door another fix just shut, the system reopens it, retrieves what it needs, then puts everything back the way it was.
  • ✓
    Don't discard a step that's genuinely necessary A pickup that's the actual cause of a problem still has to happen eventually — it no longer gets thrown away just for being the trigger.
07 · Why a language model

The search space is enormous, blind

The house understands 41 distinct actions and holds roughly 290 objects in an average scene. Checked blindly — every action against every object, before even considering actions that involve two objects at once — that's a lot to get through before finding one valid repair.

Something has to narrow the field before exact checking is even feasible. That narrowing step is where judgment — not more search — earns its place.

08 · Why a language model

Think of it as a doctor and a lab

🩺The language model is the doctor

It looks at the failure and suggests what might fix it — typically about three candidate actions per failure, drawn from everyday judgment about how a house actually works.

A doctor's judgment is what narrows down a huge range of possibilities to a short, plausible list. It isn't the final word.

⚗️The rule-based checker is the lab

It doesn't take the doctor's suggestions on faith. For each one, it can only answer one of two things:

Provably wrongrejected outright
Can't rule it outpasses through for the search to try
09 · Why a language model

The lab has the final word, always

A structured search explores only what the lab didn't rule out, and returns the shortest sequence that actually resolves the problem. Nothing downstream of that search asks the language model anything — it's ordinary, deterministic code from there.

The guarantee this gives you

The language model cannot make a broken repair pass. At worst, its suggestions are all rejected — and the step gets dropped, which can cost a goal, but it is never silently accepted as a fix that doesn't actually hold up. A wrong idea costs progress. It never costs correctness.

10 · Why a language model

The one honest exception — and how rare it is

There is exactly one situation where the language model's output goes straight into the plan without the lab checking it first: when a step is diagnosed as fundamentally the wrong thing to attempt, it's asked to propose a direct replacement, accepted as-is.

2 / 211
diagnosed failures took this unverified path across both evaluation sets — under 1%.

Everywhere else — the other 99%+ — every suggestion passes through the lab before anything reaches the plan.

11 · Why a language model

Judgment narrows it. Verification makes it exact.

  • A
    Judgment picks a short list From thousands of raw possibilities down to a handful of plausible ones, in a single step — no everyday common sense is built into the rules themselves.
  • B
    Verification makes it exact Nothing reaches the plan unless it genuinely, provably satisfies what's missing.
  • =
    Neither half is enough alone Judgment without checking would occasionally accept something that looks right but isn't. Checking without judgment would have to search enormously to find what a person would guess instantly.

15 · Takeaways

What the numbers actually say

  • →
    The loop fixes 85–97% of what it diagnoses — from a short list of ideas per failure, every one independently verified.The remaining gap is mostly one narrow, known weak spot, not broad unreliability.
  • →
    The "object can't do that" weak spot depends heavily on the task mixOnly 1 of 15 such patches worked on everyday tasks (driven by "go to sleep"-type tasks), but 13 of 14 worked on the hard set, which doesn't include that task type at all.

Closing

The short version

  • 1
    Most failures aren't random — the system can trace almost all of them back to one exact, identifiable cause.
  • 2
    A language model supplies judgment about what makes sense; formal rules supply the guarantee that only a genuinely valid fix ever gets used — and that guarantee holds for over 99% of every repair attempted.
  • 3
    That combination is what turns a plan that breaks constantly into one that recovers on its own, in 9 out of 10 cases, across two very different task sets.
